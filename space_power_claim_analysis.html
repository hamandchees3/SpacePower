Scientific plausibility check

Power plants in space?

The tweet gets one thing surprisingly right: Starlink has already industrialized a nontrivial amount of solar electric hardware in orbit. The leap from “satellites power themselves” to “turn off fossil power plants by beaming energy home” is where the orders of magnitude hide.

Claim analyzed
“Two more 10× increases” from a ~1 GW Gen3 Starlink solar-power level would give enough power in space to beam excess microwaves to ground stations and shut off fossil fuel power plants.

Bottom line

Not with two more 10× jumps.

Using the tweet’s own ladder — 10 MW → 100 MW → 1 GW → 10 GW → 100 GW of in-space electrical power — and then applying a reasonable microwave-to-grid chain, the delivered power is roughly 25–55 GW. That is about 1–3% of today’s fossil electricity generation, not enough to “bam” the fossil fleet off the grid.

Starlink power estimate Mostly plausible as onboard solar electricity, if interpreted as average-ish power rather than nameplate peak.
Microwave beaming Physically real, but it requires purpose-built transmitters, rectennas, spectrum control, safety systems, and usually huge apertures.
Two 10× steps Off by roughly 20–50× for fossil electricity after beaming losses, and more like 100×+ if the target is broader fossil energy.

Growth ladder vs. actual targets

The tweet’s ladder is internally understandable: every generation is roughly an order of magnitude more onboard solar electric capacity. But the world’s fossil electricity fleet is measured in terawatts, not gigawatts.

Parameter check: microwave beaming

Microwave power transmission is not magic; it is aperture physics. Beam diameter scales roughly as:

spot diameter ≈ 2.44 × wavelength × range / transmitter diameter

At 5.8 GHz, a 750 m transmitter in GEO has a first-null footprint of about 6 km. That lines up with NASA-style reference rectennas. A Starlink-sized aperture from LEO has a much smaller range, but also drastically less power per spacecraft and a fast-moving target. It is a communications constellation, not a power satellite swarm.

A one-off 40 kW satellite can be beamed safely only by spreading it thinly. A useful grid plant wants gigawatts, which means a deliberately designed transmitter and a deliberately designed ground receiver.

Interpretation

What would “turn off fossil power plants” require?

Using ~2.1 TW as the fossil-electricity replacement target, the required in-space electrical power is:

P_space ≈ 2.1 TW / η_beam-to-grid

At 40% beaming-to-grid efficiency, that is about 5.2 TW in space. Starting from 1 GW, that is 3.7 tenfold increases, not two. To have “ten times more power in space than fossil electricity” would require about 21 TW in space, or 4.3 tenfold increases from 1 GW.

For a NASA-style 2 GW delivered SBSP plant, replacing ~2.1 TW would mean about 1,000 such plants. If each used a 4–6 km diameter rectenna, the receptor land area alone is roughly 13,000–29,000 km². That is large but not continent-sized; the harder part is likely orbital construction, cost, reliability, regulation, and managing gigawatt microwave beams.

Why Starlink is the wrong architecture

Starlink satellites are solar-powered because every satellite must run radios, computers, propulsion, and batteries. That does not make them solar power satellites in the grid sense.

A grid SBSP system needs: solar collection, conversion to coherent microwave or laser energy, pointing, safe transmission, a large rectifying antenna on the ground, grid interconnection, and usually a high-capacity orbit. NASA’s decomposition has exactly those six functions: collect, convert, transmit, receive, convert, deliver.

LEO has a nice short range for diffraction, but each satellite moves across the sky in minutes and spends a significant fraction of each orbit in eclipse. GEO is ugly to launch to but looks nearly stationary from Earth and can see the sun almost continuously. That is why classic SBSP concepts usually live in GEO or use very carefully choreographed constellations.

Self-critique and failure modes

Most favorable interpretation: If the tweet only means “SpaceX is rapidly scaling solar-electric hardware in space,” it is directionally insightful. The Starlink array-area math supports the 10 MW and 100 MW class claims, and a gigawatt-class future constellation is not physically silly.

Least favorable interpretation: If it means two more 10× improvements from a 1 GW Gen3 network can replace fossil power plants, it is off by orders of magnitude. Two more 10× jumps gives 100 GW in space; after transmission losses that is a few tens of GW on the grid.

Uncertainties that could move the answer: actual V3 array area and operating power; Starship launch cost and cadence; PV efficiency and degradation; whether one can manufacture ultra-light kilometer-scale phased arrays; regulatory limits on microwave power density; rectenna siting; and whether future demand is “replace fossil electricity” or “replace fossil primary energy after electrification.” None of these uncertainties rescue the two-10× claim. They decide whether SBSP is merely very hard or economically deranged.
